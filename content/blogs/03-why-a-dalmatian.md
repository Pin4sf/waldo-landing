# We put a Dalmatian on it, and we would like to explain ourselves

Every AI product launching right now has the same logo. A gradient. Some kind of orb, sphere, or star. Purple to blue if they are feeling serious, orange to pink if they are feeling friendly. You could swap any two of them and nobody would file a complaint.

We have a dog.

Not as a joke, though it is a little bit funny, and not because we ran out of gradients. Because the thing we are building sits closer to you than software normally gets, and software that sits that close needs a shape you can recognise.

## The problem with an orb

Waldo is going to move your meetings. It is going to read the signals your wearable collected overnight. It will occasionally tell you that today is not the day for the hard conversation.

You have to have a relationship with something that does that. Not a friendship, nothing weird, but some working model in your head of what it is like. What it will and will not do. Whether it is going to nag you. Whether it is on your side.

An orb gives you nothing to hold. So people default to the only model they have, which is the chatbot, and the chatbot model is wrong in a specific and damaging way: it makes you think you have to ask. The entire point is that you do not have to ask.

A dog fixes that in one move. Everybody already knows what a good dog does. It notices things before you do. It is at the door before you have picked up the keys. It does not require instructions to be useful and it does not need credit afterwards.

That is the product. We did not have to explain a single word of it.

## Why this dog specifically

A Golden Retriever would have been the easy call. Warm, universally liked, extremely safe.

Also wrong. Golden Retrievers are lovely and famously not paying attention.

Dalmatians are the ones that lived in firehouses. Not as decoration. They ran ahead of horse-drawn fire engines as living sirens, guarded the horses at the scene, and helped keep them calm in the noise. Alert, useful, and alongside the work. That is quite specifically the job.

And then there are the spots.

This is the part where the branding actually earns its keep, because the whole product is about noticing individual things and then connecting them. A single observation. Then another. Then enough of them that a shape appears. In the app we call each observation a Spot. The longer-term idea is that enough linked Spots become a Constellation: a map of your own patterns you have never seen before.

The dog's coat is the data model. We are aware of how convenient that is. We did not plan it and we are not giving it back.

## Taste is a feature, and here is the boring argument for it

Design people say taste matters and product people nod politely and then ask about conversion.

The real argument is narrower than "beautiful things are nice." It is this: when software makes a decision on your behalf, your willingness to let it keep doing that is almost entirely a trust judgment, and trust judgments begin on feel, before much evidence arrives.

You have felt this. An app sends a notification crowded with exclamation marks and a fire emoji telling you to keep your streak alive, and something in you closes slightly. Nothing about the underlying maths changed. You just learned what kind of thing you are dealing with.

So the character is not paint on top of the product. It is the part of the product that decides whether the rest gets used. We spend real time on it because a Waldo that feels like a needy engagement machine does not get to move your Thursday, no matter how good the model underneath is.

Which is also why the tone is what it is. No exclamation marks. No congratulating you. No congratulating itself. Waldo does the thing, says why in one line, leaves you the undo, and stops. Capable, restrained, slightly amused. If it did the job well it does not need to mention it.

## The dog does not talk

One rule we hold hard: Waldo the character never speaks.

The illustration is always doing something. Waiting, watching, already moved. Never with a speech bubble, never pointing at a chart like a mascot in a bank advert. The words come from the product in plain language. The dog provides the presence.

The moment the dog starts talking you have a cartoon, and you cannot take a serious observation from a cartoon. Character has to survive the serious moments. When Waldo tells you your recovery has been sliding, that message has to land like a calm, capable presence, not like a puppy with a clipboard.

The other rule: the character cannot make the action harder to understand. The Patrol, The Fetch, The Adjustment, and The Brief each name a specific thing Waldo does. When you are tired in the morning, you should not have to decode a joke to find out how you slept. The character lives in the behaviour and the drawing. The labels stay clear.

## It has to work when it is tiny

Everything gets designed favicon-first. If the mark does not read in a browser tab next to everything else you have open, it does not ship.

That constraint is unglamorous and it kills most ideas immediately. Detailed illustration turns to grey mush. Anything relying on the bow tie fails. What survives is the silhouette and the asymmetric spots, which is why the mark ended up as simple as it did.

The same test runs everywhere else. It has to work as an app icon, as a few characters in a terminal, as a small element on a card, as a full character in an illustration, and eventually as an object on a desk. The version of Waldo you would find on a sticker and the version watching your Tuesday have to be visibly the same animal, or the whole thing is just decoration.

That is the ambition, anyway. Physical Waldo is a sketch and a few conversations at this point, not a product. Nobody is shipping a plush toy yet.

## The short version

We are asking people to let software act for them using data from inside their own bodies. That is a big ask, and it is not won with a feature list.

Every other health brand talks to the version of you that is trying. The one with a plan, and a streak, and a fresh start on Monday. We are talking to the version of you that is tired of trying and would quite like something competent to take a few things off the pile.

A dog was the honest way to say that.
